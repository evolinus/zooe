# intro
Two loci on the same chromosome, a distance $r$ apart. Part 1 builds an association between A₁ and B₁ and lets recombination pull it apart. Part 2 drops two beneficial mutations into different individuals and counts how often the population manages to keep both. This guide asks how long associations last and what they cost.

# objectives core
- Define linkage disequilibrium $D$ and read it from the gamete square.
- Predict how quickly an association decays at different recombination rates.
- Contrast Hardy–Weinberg (reached in one generation) with linkage equilibrium (only approached).
- Explain Hill–Robertson interference: why two good mutations on different chromosomes compete.

# objectives ext
- Use $D_t$ = $D$<sub>0</sub>(1 − $r$)<sup>$t$</sup> and $t$<sub>½</sub> ≈ 0.69/$r$ to predict decay.
- Use 2$Nrf$<sub>0</sub>² to predict how fast the double mutant is made, and how that depends on $N$.
- Connect $r$ = 0 to asexual reproduction and the advantage of sex.

# step core 8 | Free recombination
set: Part 1. Recombination $r$ = **0.5**, $D$<sub>0</sub>/$D$<sub>max</sub> = 1, $p$ = 0.5.
predict: At $r$ = 0.5 the loci behave as if on different chromosomes. How many generations until most of the association is gone?
run: Press **Auto-play**.
look: the gamete square, the decay curve, and the $D$ readout.
explain: What does $r$ = 0.5 mean physically, and why does the association halve every generation?

# step core 10 | Tight linkage
set: $r$ = **0.005**. Press **Reset to generation 0**.
predict: After 60 generations, what fraction of the starting association will be left?
run: **Auto-play** for about 60 generations.
explain: Why does tight linkage let an association persist? Name a real situation where alleles at nearby loci are found together far more often than chance would predict.

# step core 12 | Two good mutations
set: Part 2: $N$ = 50, $s$ = 0.20, starting frequency 0.050, 200 replicates. Use $r$ = 0.010 from Part 1.
predict: Each mutation on its own would usually spread. In what share of runs will the population keep *both*? Will that share be higher or lower than with free recombination?
run: Press **Watch one population**, then **Run all replicates**.
look: the bars in *How often both mutations survive*, at your $r$ against free recombination.
explain: Why are the two mutations rivals rather than allies until recombination puts them on one chromosome?

# step core 5 | One generation, or never quite
set: Nothing to change.
predict: Hardy–Weinberg proportions are reached in one generation. Is linkage equilibrium?
explain: Explain the difference, in terms of what random mating can and cannot reshuffle in one generation.

# step ext 10 | Half-lives
set: Part 1: $r$ = 0.01, 0.05 and 0.20 in turn.
predict: Calculate $t$<sub>½</sub> = ln 2/[−ln(1 − $r$)] for each.
run: Auto-play from generation 0 and note when $D$ falls to half of $D$<sub>0</sub>.
explain: Why is the approximation 0.69/$r$ good at small $r$ and poor at large $r$?

# step ext 12 | Population size and interference
set: Part 2, $r$ = 0.01. $N$ = 50, then $N$ = **500**.
predict: The expected number of double-mutant chromosomes made per generation is about 2$Nrf$<sub>0</sub>². Calculate it at both sizes. Will interference be stronger or weaker in the larger population?
run: **Run all replicates** at each $N$.
explain: Why does a larger population get both mutations onto one chromosome more often? What does this say about adaptation in small populations?

# step ext 8 | No recombination at all
set: Part 2 with $r$ = **0**.
predict: What share of runs will keep both mutations?
run: Run all replicates.
explain: An asexual lineage lives at $r$ = 0 across its whole genome. Use this result to state one advantage of sex, and connect it to the Reproduction Room.

# questions
- [core] Why might two alleles be found together more often than expected in a population without any selection favouring the combination?
- [core] A disease allele is in strong linkage disequilibrium with a marker. Why is that useful to a geneticist?
- [core] Explain in one paragraph why the one-locus rooms are a simplification.
- [ext] Genetic hitchhiking: how can a neutral allele rise to high frequency because of selection at a linked locus? Use $D$ in your answer.
- [ext] Why do regions of low recombination in many genomes carry less neutral diversity?
- [ext] The room uses multiplicative fitness, so there is no epistasis. How would positive or negative epistasis between A₁ and B₁ change Part 2?

# check
- **Step 1.** The association halves each generation: after five generations about 3% is left. $r$ = 0.5 means a recombinant gamete is as likely as a parental one.
- **Step 2.** About (1 − 0.005)⁶⁰ ≈ 0.74 of the association remains. Nearby alleles stay together for many generations — haplotype blocks in human genomes, for example.
- **Step 3.** Both are kept noticeably less often at $r$ = 0.01 than with free recombination; expect roughly half the runs against about seven in ten, give or take a few points from one batch of replicates to the next.
- **Step 4.** Random mating rebuilds genotypes from allele frequencies at once, but an association between loci is only broken by recombination events, a fraction $r$ per generation.
- **Step 5.** About 69, 13.5 and 3.1 generations. At large $r$, −ln(1 − $r$) is no longer close to $r$.
- **Step 6.** About 0.0025 copies per generation at $N$ = 50 and 0.025 at $N$ = 500: the larger population builds the double mutant ten times as fast, so it keeps both more often.
- **Step 7.** None: with no recombination the double mutant can never be made, and one of the two mutations is always lost.
