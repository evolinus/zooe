# intro
Two founders reproduce side by side for four generations. On the left, every individual makes two exact clones; on the right, two parents make two offspring, each with one recombined copy from each parent. This guide asks what each way of reproducing buys, and what it costs.

# objectives core
- Describe how asexual and sexual reproduction make the next generation.
- Predict head counts and numbers of distinct genotypes on each side.
- Identify a crossover and explain how recombination creates new combinations.
- State the two-fold cost of sex and the advantage it buys.

# objectives ext
- Calculate how the cost of sex compounds over generations.
- Compare the variation created by crossing over with that from independent assortment (2<sup>$n$</sup> gametes).
- Use the full-sib recursion to explain why this sexual line understates what sex generates.

# questions
- [core] Why is the two-fold cost of sex sometimes called the "cost of males"?
- [core] A disease that kills one genotype arrives at generation 4. What happens to each side?
- [core] Bdelloid rotifers have reproduced asexually for millions of years. Why is that considered a puzzle?
- [ext] Explain Muller's ratchet and why it bites hardest in small asexual populations.
- [ext] Summarise the Red Queen hypothesis for the maintenance of sex. What kind of evidence supports it?
- [ext] Why does recombination need genetic variation to be useful? What would sex do in a population where every individual was identical?

# check
- **Step 1.** Asexual: 2 → 4 → 8 → 16. Sexual: 2 throughout. Each asexual individual reproduces alone; a sexual pair must share its offspring.
- **Step 2.** Asexual: still only the 2 founding genotypes. Sexual: 2 individuals, but both new genotypes that never existed before, because crossover positions vary continuously.
- **Step 3.** The half that changes shade is one chromosome copy built from pieces of both of its parent's copies; asexual copies are never broken and rejoined.
- **Step 4.** Stable environments favour sheer numbers (asexual); changing ones favour variety (sexual). Facultative switching takes both advantages.
- **Step 5.** Ratios 8, 1,024 and about a million. With paternal care the cost falls, because a male's investment raises the number of offspring that survive.
- **Step 6.** 2²³ ≈ 8.4 million gametes from assortment alone in a human, and 2 here. Here almost all variety comes from where the crossover falls; in a real genome it comes from both.
- **Step 7.** $H$ = 0.75, 0.625, 0.5. Heterozygosity falls by about a fifth each generation in a full-sib line, so the variety recombination can work with drains away.
