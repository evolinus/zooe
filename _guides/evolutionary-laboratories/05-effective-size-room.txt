# intro
The Drift Room's population is ideal: the same size every generation, every individual equally likely to be a parent, mating at random. This room breaks those promises one at a time — a size that changes, unequal numbers of the two sexes, mating with relatives — and measures what each costs in lost diversity. The $N$ of the ideal population that would lose diversity at the same rate is the effective population size, $N_e$.

# objectives core
- Define the effective population size as the size of an ideal population that loses genetic diversity at the same rate.
- Explain why a population that changes size has an $N_e$ close to the harmonic mean of its sizes, and why a short bottleneck therefore matters so much.
- Explain why the rarer sex limits $N_e$ when the numbers of breeding males and females differ.
- Distinguish inbreeding as non-random mating (a heterozygote deficit within the population) from inbreeding as relatedness in a small population (drift seen from the individual).

# objectives ext
- Calculate the harmonic mean of a size schedule and use $H_t$ = $H$<sub>0</sub>(1 − 1/2$N_e$)<sup>$t$</sup> to predict a run.
- Use $N_e$ = 4$N_m$$N_f$/($N_m$ + $N_f$) to show that $N_e$ can never exceed four times the number of the rarer sex.
- Derive $F$ = $σ$/(2 − $σ$) under partial selfing and explain why selfing also lowers $N_e$ to $N$/(1 + $F$).

# step core 8 | Bigger on average, poorer in diversity

# step ext 10 | What selfing does to the gene pool
set: Part 3, $N$ = 50. Selfing $σ$ = 0.5, then 1.
predict: Calculate $F$ = $σ$/(2 − $σ$) and $N_e$ = $N$/(1 + $F$) for each. Will the purple gene-diversity line fall faster than the red dotted line of a randomly mating population of 50?

# questions
- [core] A population was 10,000 for a century, except for one hard winter that left 20 survivors. Which number matters more for its genetic diversity today, and why?
- [core] Why does adding more females to a herd with two breeding males barely raise its effective size?
- [core] A selfing plant population and an outcrossing one have the same allele frequencies. How do their genotype frequencies differ, and which will lose diversity faster?
- [ext] A conservation plan counts 2,000 adults in a population whose numbers swing tenfold between good and bad years, with one male in five breeding. Explain, without exact numbers, why its $N_e$ may be in the low hundreds or below.
- [ext] Every room after this one uses $N$. Should it be read as the census or as $N_e$? Give an example where the difference changes whether selection or drift is in charge.
- [ext] Inbreeding in a small population and drift are sometimes called two descriptions of one process. Explain what that means, using the gold line of Part 3 at $σ$ = 0.

# check
- **Step 1.** About 145, the harmonic mean — nearer 10 than 476 in its effect. The schedule predicts $H$<sub>100</sub> = 0.352 against 0.450 from the arithmetic mean; runs land around 0.35 with *Ne from the run* usually between 130 and 160. Loci fixed or lost in the crash stay there: the recovery brings back individuals, not alleles.
- **Step 2.** The constant population of 200 keeps more: $H$<sub>100</sub> = 0.5 × (1 − 1/400)<sup>100</sup> ≈ 0.389, against about 0.352 for the bottleneck. The harmonic means (200 against 145) put them in the right order; the arithmetic means (200 against 476) put them the wrong way round. Five generations at 10 contribute 5/10 = 0.5 to Σ1/$N$, the other 95 only 95/500 = 0.19.
- **Step 3.** $N_e$ = 50, 7.7 and 7.8. At 25:25 about 0.18 of the diversity is left after 100 generations; with 2 males almost none (expected 0.0007). Every offspring has one of the two males as its father, so half the gene pool passes through two individuals each generation, however many females there are.
- **Step 4.** At $σ$ = 0 the indigo line hovers near 0 and the genome panel shows heterozygotes scattered evenly, yet the gold line rises to about 0.63 (1 − 0.99<sup>100</sup>): in a population of 50 everyone becomes related, which is drift. At $σ$ = 1 the indigo line goes to 1 within about five generations (0.5, 0.75, 0.88, 0.94, 0.97) and the genomes turn pale everywhere: no heterozygotes, with no change in allele frequency needed.
- **Step 5.** Boom & bust: arithmetic mean 510, harmonic mean 100/(50/1,000 + 50/20) = 39.2. Predicted $H$<sub>100</sub> 0.139 from the harmonic mean, 0.453 from the arithmetic. Expansion: harmonic mean 99.5 against an arithmetic 744, most of the loss in the first thirty generations. Decline: harmonic 76 against arithmetic 259, the loss accelerating at the end. A census in a peak year overstates $N_e$ more than tenfold.
- **Step 6.** $N_e$ = 10, 16 and 19; the limit is 4 × 5 = 20. $N_e$ = 4$N_m$ · $N_f$/($N_m$ + $N_f$), and the fraction is always below 1. Elephant seals, red deer, lekking birds, and livestock bred from a few sires are the classic cases.
- **Step 7.** $σ$ = 0.5: $F$ = 1/3, $N_e$ = 37.5; $σ$ = 1: $F$ = 1, $N_e$ = 25. The purple line falls below the red dotted one in both, to about 0.13 and 0.07 at generation 100, against 0.18. Two genes in different offspring share a parent with probability 1/$N$ and are then identical with probability (1 + $F$)/2, so selfing raises the per-generation loss. Under heavy selfing the 100 loci share their carriers' histories and are not independent, so a single run is noisier.
