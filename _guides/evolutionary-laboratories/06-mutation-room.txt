# intro
Four wheels, one per character of a fish, decide which derived state a mutation produces. Some slices are larger than others: some mutations are simply easier to make. The new alleles then enter a population of $N$ as single copies. This guide separates the two questions the room keeps apart: which mutations appear, and what becomes of them.

# objectives core
- Distinguish the probability that a mutation *arises* from the probability that it *spreads*.
- Predict that new neutral alleles are usually lost, and that selection raises the chance of favoured ones.
- Show, from the wheels, that selection does not change which mutations are produced.
- Explain the phrase "mutation proposes, selection disposes" with evidence from the room.

# objectives ext
- Use $u$(1/$N$) = (1 − e<sup>−2$s$</sup>)/(1 − e<sup>−2$Ns$</sup>) to predict fixation bars, and judge them against sampling error.
- Explain why the ratio between selected and neutral fixation grows with $N$.
- Recognise mutation bias in the wheels' slice sizes and say why it is not the same thing as a directed mutation.

# routes
- **35-minute practical (BSc):** steps 1–4 and questions 1–3.
- **Lecture demonstration:** step 2, freezing the wheels on screen while the class watches the ★ slices.
- **MSc:** steps 5–7 and questions 4–6 (about 30 minutes).

# step core 8 | Spin, and watch four new alleles
set: Population size $N$ = 50, *Fate of the alleles*: **Neutral**.
predict: Each of the four derived alleles starts as one copy among 50. How many of the four will be fixed by the end of the run?
run: Press **Spin — mutate all four characters**, then watch the *Derived alleles* panel. Spin three more times.
look: the four frequency lines, and the fish after $G$ generations.
explain: Was your prediction right? Connect the result to what you saw in the Fate Room.

# step core 10 | Turn selection on
set: *Fate of the alleles*: **Selection**. The stream favours a slender body, blue colour, a long tail and short fins (marked ★). Raise $s$ to about 0.2.
predict: Two things to predict separately. (a) Will the ★ slices of the wheels get bigger? (b) Will the favoured alleles do better in the population?
run: Spin several times. Watch the wheels while you raise and lower $s$, and watch the frequency lines after each spin.
look: the size of the ★ slices, and which lines climb and which are pushed down.
explain: Which of your two predictions came true? What does that tell you about where selection acts?

# step core 10 | A hundred runs of the same four mutations
set: Selection on, $s$ = 0.1, $N$ = 50. Spin once.
predict: For the four alleles you just drew, predict the height of each bar in *Fixed in 100 runs*. Which will be near the dashed neutral line at 1/$N$, and which above or below it?
run: Press **Run 100 Simulations**. Then switch back to Neutral and press it again.
look: each bar against the dashed line at 1/$N$ = 0.02.
explain: Why did one run (step 2) tell you so much less than a hundred?

# step core 5 | Put it in one sentence
set: Nothing to change.
predict: Before writing, look back at your answers to steps 2 and 3.
explain: Write one sentence that uses the words *mutation*, *selection*, *origin* and *fate*, and that someone who has not seen this room would understand.

# step ext 12 | Predicting the bars
set: $N$ = 50, Selection on. Use $s$ = 0.05, 0.1 and 0.2 in turn, with the same spin.
predict: Calculate $u$(1/$N$) for a favoured allele at each $s$. How big is the sampling error of a bar from 100 runs?

run: **Run 100 Simulations** at each $s$ and read the bars for the favoured alleles.
explain: Do the bars agree with your calculation within sampling error? Why is $u$ ≈ 2$s$ a reasonable shortcut here?

# step ext 10 | Population size changes the contrast
set: $s$ = 0.1, Selection on. $N$ = 50, then $N$ = **300**.
predict: What will happen to the neutral line at 1/$N$? To the bars of favoured alleles? To unfavoured ones?
run: **Run 100 Simulations** at each $N$.
explain: The ratio of selected to neutral fixation is about 2$Ns$. Why does a larger population sort mutations more sharply, even though it receives the same four mutations?

# step ext 10 | Mutation bias
set: Neutral. Reset between spins if you like.
predict: Pick one wheel, for example body colour. If you spin twenty times, how often will each derived state come up? Use the slice sizes.
run: Spin twenty times and tally the outcomes for that wheel.
explain: Some states are produced more often than others. Is that a bias towards *useful* states? How could a strong mutation bias still influence which traits evolve, even though selection does not set it?

# questions
- [core] "Bacteria mutate to become resistant when they meet an antibiotic." What is wrong with this sentence, and how would you rewrite it?
- [core] Why is the observation that a favoured allele usually wins *after* it appears not evidence that the environment *caused* it to appear?
- [core] The room assumes one mutation per character. Real mutation rates are around 10⁻⁸ per site per generation. Which of your conclusions would change with realistic rates, and which would not?
- [ext] Explain the Luria–Delbrück fluctuation test, and say what it showed about the relationship between mutation and selection.
- [ext] The room's wheels have unequal slices. Give a real example of mutation bias (for example transitions against transversions, or CpG mutability) and discuss whether it can shape evolution.
- [ext] The neutral theory says most substitutions are neutral even though most adaptive change is selected. How do the bars in step 6 help reconcile those two statements?

# check
- **Step 1.** Most spins end with all four lines on the floor. Each new neutral allele fixes with probability 1/50, so on average 0.08 of the four fix per spin.
- **Step 2.** The ★ slices never change size, whatever $s$ is. Favoured alleles climb more often and the rest are pushed down: selection acts after the mutation exists.
- **Step 3.** Neutral bars sit near 0.02. With $s$ = 0.1, favoured bars rise to about 0.18 and unfavoured ones fall towards zero.
- **Step 4.** A good answer: "Mutation decides which variants appear, blindly; selection only changes the fate of a variant once it has appeared."
- **Step 5.** $u$ ≈ 0.096, 0.18 and 0.33 for $s$ = 0.05, 0.1 and 0.2. A bar from 100 runs has a standard error of √($u$(1 − $u$)/100), about 0.03–0.05 here.
- **Step 6.** At $N$ = 300 the neutral line falls to about 0.003, favoured bars stay near 0.18, and unfavoured ones vanish. The contrast between selected and neutral grows with $N$.
- **Step 7.** The tallies follow the slice sizes. The bias is in what is *easy to make*, not in what is useful; but a state produced often is offered to selection more often, so bias can matter when several routes would serve.
