# intro
The Neighbours Room's two competitors and the Predator Room's predator in one community. Every run is simulated twice — as you set it and with the predator deleted — so a removal experiment is always on screen. This guide asks when a predator changes who survives, and why.

# objectives core
- Explain keystone predation: a predator that maintains coexistence by feeding on the dominant competitor.
- Use the predator-free counterfactual to measure a predator's effect on each species.
- Explain apparent competition, and why it can look exactly like resource competition.
- Show that a predator only acts as a keystone when it prefers the species that would otherwise win.

# objectives ext
- Apply the three-species invasion criterion and check it against the room.
- Find how unequal the attack rates must be for keystone predation to work.
- Read the *Tolerance of predation* panel, $r$/$a$.

# step ext 12 | The invasion criterion

Calculate both, then B's growth rate when rare: $r$<sub>B</sub>(1 − $α$<sub>BA</sub>$N$<sub>A</sub>*/$K$<sub>B</sub>) − $a$<sub>B</sub>$P$*. Can B invade?

# step ext 10 | How lopsided must the plate be?
set: Keystone predation; leave $a$<sub>B</sub> = 0.002 and lower $a$<sub>A</sub> step by step: 0.008, 0.007, 0.006, 0.005.

# questions
- [core] Paine removed the starfish *Pisaster* from rocky shores and diversity collapsed. Explain his result using this room.
- [core] Two snail species show opposite trends in abundance. Give two explanations, one involving competition and one not, and suggest a field test to tell them apart.
- [core] Why might removing a predator to "protect" a prey species backfire?
- [ext] Holt (1977) introduced apparent competition. Give a real example, for instance from biological control or invasive species.
- [ext] The predator here never switches to the commoner prey. How would prey switching change keystone effects?
- [ext] Explain how indirect effects make it hard to predict the consequences of removing one species from a food web.

# check
- **Step 1.** All three persist; without the predator (dashed lines) A excludes B. The predator helps B by holding A down.
- **Step 2.** B disappears. A removal experiment reveals the predator's role only because it was compared with the intact community.
- **Step 3.** A eliminates B, with zero competition: A feeds the predator, and the predator, attacking B harder, drives B out.
- **Step 4.** No: preferring B, the predator hastens B's exclusion. A keystone must prefer the species that would otherwise win.
- **Step 5.** $N$<sub>A</sub>* = 0.6/(0.4 × 0.008) = 187.5, $P$* ≈ 45.8, and B's growth when rare ≈ 0.148 − 0.092 ≈ +0.06: B can invade.
- **Step 6.** B's invasion rate turns negative between about 0.007 and 0.006, well before the attack rates are equal: the predator must press much harder on A.
- **Step 7.** A tolerates at most 0.5/0.008 ≈ 62 predators, B 0.5/0.002 = 250. A on its own supports about 46 predators — close to its own limit, so A is held well below its carrying capacity, while B barely notices that many predators. That is what opens the door for B.
