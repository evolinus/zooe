# intro
A resource is supplied at rate $S$, decays at rate $l$ and is eaten by consumers, whose births depend on how much they eat. Nothing in the equations is a carrying capacity — and yet one appears. This guide asks where it comes from and what sets it.

# objectives core
- Explain how a carrying capacity emerges from a supplied and consumed resource.
- Define $R$* as the resource level at which a consumer breaks even, and read it from the room.
- Predict that richer supply raises the number of consumers but not $R$*.
- Explain why carrying capacity is a property of a species *in an environment*, not of the species alone.

# objectives ext
- Calculate $R$* = $m$/($ea$) and $N$* = ($S$ − $lR$*)/($aR$*) by hand and check them.
- Predict when the approach to equilibrium is a damped spiral and when it is smooth.
- State the condition $S$/$l$ > $R$* for the consumer to persist.

# questions
- [core] A lake receives twice as much phosphorus after farming intensifies upstream. According to this room, what happens to the algae-grazing zooplankton, and to the phosphorus level?
- [core] Why is it misleading to say "the carrying capacity of a cow is 1 hectare"?
- [core] Which consumer is the better competitor: one that eats a lot, or one with a low $R$*? Explain.
- [ext] The resource here is abiotic, like a nutrient in a chemostat. What changes if the resource is itself a population that grows?
- [ext] The functional response is linear. What would a saturating response do to $R$* and to the stability of the equilibrium?
- [ext] Explain how this model relates to the logistic: under what conditions does the consumer's dynamics look logistic?

# check
- **Step 1.** The resource crashes as consumers build up, then both settle in damped oscillations: $R$* = 50 and $N$* = 35. Each turn of the consumer curve coincides with the resource crossing $R$*.
- **Step 2.** $R$* stays at 50; $N$* rises to about 72.5. Supply does not appear in $R$*.
- **Step 3.** After the shift the consumers fall to about 16 while the resource returns to 50: the consumers track the supply, the resource is held at $R$*.
- **Step 4.** $R$* halves to 25; $N$* rises to about 73. A low $R$* means surviving on less.
- **Step 5.** $R$* = 0.32/(0.4 × 0.008) = 100; $N$* = (15 − 2)/(0.008 × 100) ≈ 16. $R$* depends only on the consumer; $N$* also on the supply.
- **Step 6.** The default spirals in ($m$ > $l$, and 225 < 896). With $l$ = 0.4 > $m$ there is no spiral: the consumers settle at $N$* = 50 without circling. (Supply was raised so that $S$/$l$ stays above $R$*.)
- **Step 7.** $S$/$l$ = 20 < $R$* = 50, so the consumer cannot persist and dies out.
