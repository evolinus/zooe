# intro
Several sites, each with its own community. $γ$ is the diversity of all of them pooled, $α$ that of a typical site, and $β$ = $γ$/$α$ how many completely distinct sites they amount to. This guide asks how a landscape's diversity is built from its parts, and what happens when the sites drift apart.

# objectives core
- Define $α$, $β$ and $γ$ diversity and use $γ$ = $α$ × $β$.
- Show that local diversity does not predict regional diversity.
- Explain how the choice of measure (richness, Shannon, Simpson) changes $β$.
- Show that neutral drift in isolated sites creates turnover, and that immigration from a shared pool removes it.

# objectives ext
- Calculate $α$, $β$ and $γ$ for a small landscape by hand.
- Connect $β$ to $F$<sub>ST</sub> in the Migration Room.
- Discuss the several-small-or-single-large question, and what the model leaves out of it.

# step core 10 | Identical sites and distinct sites

# step core 8 | The measure matters
set: Preset **Nested**, then **Gradient**. For each, switch the measure between Richness, Shannon and Simpson.
predict: Nested sites differ only in their rare species. By which measure will their $β$ be largest? And for the gradient, where each site has its own commonest species?
run: Read $β$ under each measure for both presets.
explain: What does the direction of the difference tell you about *which* species turn over?

# step core 10 | Drift makes turnover

run: Press **Run**.

explain: Why do sites that start alike lose *different* species?

# step core 7 | Several small or single large?
set: The run you just made.

run: Read *Several small or single large?* and the middle panel's two lines through time.
explain: Under pure chance, which wins in the end, and why? What real risks to small sites does the model leave out?

# questions
- [core] Two regions each have an average of 10 species per site. Can one region have far more species overall? Explain with $β$.
- [core] Why is $β$ in this room counted in "sites" rather than in species?
- [core] A conservation agency must choose between one large reserve and four small ones of the same total area. What does this room say, and what does it leave out?
- [ext] Additive partitions ($β$ = $γ$ − $α$) and pairwise indices (Jaccard, Sørensen) also measure turnover. Why can't values from different conventions be compared?
- [ext] Sampling inflates $β$. Explain why, using the Diversity Room's lesson about richness.
- [ext] Real turnover is mostly driven by environmental differences rather than drift. How would you distinguish the two in field data?

# check
- **Step 1.** Identical: $γ$ = $α$, $β$ = 1. Complete turnover: $γ$ = 4$α$, $β$ = $N$ = 4.
- **Step 2.** Nested: $β$ largest by richness (the sites differ only in rare species, which Shannon and Simpson discount). Gradient: largest by Simpson (each site's commonest species is its own).
- **Step 3.** $α$ falls as each site loses species; $β$ climbs because the sites lose different species by chance.
- **Step 4.** The large site often leads early, but with no immigration the small sites together end up holding more: each ends with a species of its own, the large site with one. Edge effects, area-demanding species and single disasters are all missing.
- **Step 5.** Richness: $γ$ = 3, $α$ = 2, $β$ = 1.5. Shannon: $γ$ ≈ 2.83, $α$ = 2, $β$ ≈ 1.41. Simpson: $γ$ ≈ 2.67, $α$ = 2, $β$ ≈ 1.33.
- **Step 6.** Immigration from the pooled landscape pulls every site towards the same community, so $β$ falls towards 1.
- **Step 7.** $β$ falls as $m$ rises, just as $F$<sub>ST</sub> falls with migration: both measure how different places are, for species and for alleles.
