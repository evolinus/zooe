# intro
A real community has dozens of species, and ecologists sum it up in a number. This room builds a community, samples it the way a field ecologist must, and lets it drift with no species better than another. This guide asks what each number measures, how far a sample can be trusted, and what keeps a community diverse.

# objectives core
- Distinguish richness from evenness, and explain why both make up diversity.
- Compare richness, Shannon's and Simpson's effective numbers, and say how each weighs rare species.
- Explain why richness estimated from a sample is biased low, and why comparing samples of different size is unsafe.
- Show that neutral drift removes species and that immigration can stop the loss.

# objectives ext
- Calculate $H$′, $D$ and the Hill numbers by hand and check them.
- Read a species-accumulation curve and relate it to sampling effort.
- Explain how community size $J$ and immigration $m$ set the diversity a neutral community settles at.

# step core 8 | Same species, different diversity
set: Step 1 of the room, $S$ = 8. Preset **Even**, then **One dominant**.
predict: In the even community, what will richness, Shannon's and Simpson's effective numbers be? In the one-dominant community, which of the three will fall furthest?
run: Choose each preset and read the panels.
look: *Effective number of species*: richness, ¹$D$ = e<sup>$H$′</sup> and ²$D$ = 1/$D$.
explain: Both communities hold the same eight species. Why are they so different in diversity?

# step core 8 | Add a rare species
set: Preset **One dominant**.
predict: You add one rare species. Which of the three numbers will change most, and which least?
run: Press **Add a rare species** (it arrives with just 2 individuals). Then drag its bar up to about 20 and watch the three numbers again.
explain: Choosing an index is choosing how much the rare species should count. Which index would you use to report a conservation success for a rare species, and which to describe the community's common core?

# step core 12 | Sampling the community
set: Step 2 of the room. Preset **Geometric** ($S$ = 8). Sample size $n$ = 20.
predict: In a sample of 20 individuals, how many of the 8 species will you find? Over 100 such samples, which estimate (richness, Shannon, Simpson) will be furthest below the truth on average?
run: Press **Take a sample**, then **Take 100 samples**.
look: *Species found as you count* and *A hundred samples against the truth*.
explain: Why can a sample miss a species but never invent one? What does that mean for richness comparisons between surveys of different effort?

# questions
- [core] Two meadows each have 20 plant species. In one, a single grass makes up 90% of the plants. Which is more diverse, and by which measure?
- [core] A student reports that site A has more species than site B. Site A was sampled for twice as long. What should you ask before accepting the conclusion?
- [core] Why might a manager care about Shannon's number rather than raw richness?
- [ext] Hubbell's neutral theory assumes all species are equivalent. What evidence would you look for to test it?
- [ext] Explain why Simpson's Σ$p$<sub>$i$</sub>² is the probability that two individuals belong to the same species, and connect it to heterozygosity.
- [ext] Rarefaction is used to compare samples of different size. Explain its logic in terms of the accumulation curve.

# check
- **Step 1.** Even: all three equal 8. One dominant: richness 8, Shannon's about 2.9, Simpson's about 1.8 — Simpson's falls furthest because it is set by the commonest species.
- **Step 2.** Richness rises by a whole species; Shannon's by about 0.2; Simpson's barely moves (about 0.05). Dragged up to 20 individuals the new species counts for more in both (about +0.8 and +0.5). Richness suits rare-species reporting; Simpson's describes the common core.
- **Step 3.** A sample of 20 usually finds 4–6 of the 8. Richness is the most biased low, Shannon's less so, Simpson's is nearly right.
- **Step 4.** With $m$ = 0 species are lost one after another and eventually one takes over. A little immigration halts the slide. It is the Drift Room with species in place of alleles.
- **Step 5.** $p$ = 0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1; $H$′ ≈ 1.28, e<sup>$H$′</sup> ≈ 3.6; $D$ = 0.30, 1/$D$ ≈ 3.3; $J$′ ≈ 0.92.
- **Step 6.** Richness climbs towards 8 as $n$ grows; Simpson's barely moves. Chao1 is one estimator of unseen species.
- **Step 7.** $J$ = 2000 holds more species: drift is slower in a larger community, so immigration keeps up with the losses.
