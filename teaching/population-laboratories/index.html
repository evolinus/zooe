Population Laboratories

Welcome — start here.

zoo_e lab

Welcome to the Population Laboratories. Eleven rooms of interactive simulations about a single question: what stops a population from growing? The first four answer it from inside the species — crowding, resources, and time lags. The next four answer it from outside: two rooms where another species eats the same food, one where another species eats you, and one where both things happen at once and the answer stops being obvious. The ninth stands back from the question to ask how a community of many species is described and measured at all. The last two spread life across a landscape: one asks how the diversity of a region is made up from the diversity of its sites, and the other changes the unit the question is asked in, counting populations instead of individuals.

How to use the rooms

Every room follows the same pattern: set the parameters with the sliders and buttons at the top, press Run, and read the panels that fill in below. The whole trajectory is computed the moment you press Run and then played back to you, so you can pause anywhere, drag the History Scrubber at the bottom of most rooms back and forth, and replay any moment you like. In the Overshoot Room it is worth using deliberately: stepping one generation at a time is what turns the cobweb diagram from a tangle into an explanation.

Each room carries its own guidance, so this page stays short. A Start here panel — the first thing in the room, above everything else — says what the room shows and suggests what to try first; a What to notice panel below the simulation says what the run was for, and points to the other rooms that take the idea further. Previous and Next at the foot of each room walk them in order. Follow those and the eleven rooms read as one argument rather than eleven separate toys.

Every room also has a printable room guide (PDF), linked at the foot of its Start here panel. It turns the room into a worksheet: you predict the outcome before each run and explain it afterwards, along a short core path for BSc students and an extension for MSc students, with times, learning objectives and discussion questions, and a last page for checking your reasoning. The overview sets all eleven side by side, with suggested routes for a course or a single session.

Between the run and the payoff sits What this model assumes: the things the room is quietly taking for granted, and — where there is one — the room that stops taking them for granted. This is worth reading. Most of these laboratories exist precisely to relax an assumption an earlier one made, so what looks like a convenient simplification in one room turns out to be the whole subject of another. It is also the honest answer to "but real populations are not like that", which is true of every model here and is the point rather than an objection.

Between the two sits the equation the room is running. Underneath it is a collapsible What each symbol means key: open it and every letter in the equation above is named, said plainly, and placed against what the earlier rooms called it. Nothing in a room requires reading the algebra — but nothing in the algebra is left unexplained either.

Some rooms are deterministic: the same settings always give exactly the same curve, because the equations contain no chance. Others draw births and deaths at random, so running twice will not give the same answer twice — the parameters set tendencies, never outcomes. Each room says which kind it is.

The small red ? buttons beside a control or a panel explain what it does, what the numbers mean, and what to look for. If something on screen is unclear, there is usually a ? next to it.

Words worth pinning down

These twenty come up again and again. Each one is underlined wherever it appears in a room — click it, anywhere in the tool, for a short explanation. They are gathered here too:

  • Population — a group of the same species, in one place, that could interbreed — the unit everything here is measured in.
  • Per-capita rate — per individual, per unit of time. Ask of every rate whether it is this or a population total.
  • Density dependence — a birth or death rate that changes with crowding. Only this can regulate a population.
  • Intrinsic rate of increase (r) — the per-capita growth rate of a population that nothing is holding back.
  • Carrying capacity (K) — the size at which births balance deaths, so the population stops changing.
  • Equilibrium — a state in which nothing is changing — and the real question is whether it attracts.
  • Intraspecific vs. interspecific competition — competition within a species versus between species. The balance between them decides every coexistence result in the lab.
  • Demographic stochasticity — the coin-flip nature of individual births and deaths, which matters most when numbers are small.
  • Phase plane — a graph of one species against another, with time nowhere on it.
  • Isocline (nullcline) — the line on a phase plane along which one species stops changing.
  • Invasion criterion — can this species increase when it is rare? Ask it of both species and you have the outcome.
  • Competitive exclusion — two species making a living in exactly the same way cannot persist together.
  • R* — the resource level at which a consumer breaks even; the lower one wins.
  • Functional response — how many prey one predator eats, as a function of how many there are.
  • Limit cycle — a permanent oscillation whose size belongs to the parameters, not to where the run started.
  • Trophic cascade — an effect that travels down a food chain and changes a level the predator never touches.
  • Niche — the conditions and resources a population needs to persist. Two species with identical niches cannot coexist.
  • Species diversity — how many species a community holds and how evenly its individuals are shared among them. Two communities with the same species can differ enormously in it.
  • Alpha, beta and gamma diversity — the diversity of a typical site, how different the sites are from each other, and the diversity of all of them together. Gamma is alpha times beta.
  • Metapopulation — a population of populations, linked by colonisation. It can persist although every one of its parts goes extinct.

The through-line

The eleven rooms fall into six parts, and each part dismantles an assumption the one before it was allowed to make. Part I takes a single population on its own: first with nothing in its way, which turns out to be impossible for long, and then with individuals getting under each other's feet, which bends the curve into the familiar S. Part II asks where that ceiling actually comes from — not a number belonging to the species, but the resource the environment can supply — and then shows that even a perfectly self-limiting population need not settle down quietly: delay the feedback by one generation and it can cycle forever, or become genuinely unpredictable while obeying a perfectly deterministic rule. Part III adds a second species that wants the same food, first as a bare coefficient that produces the four classic outcomes of competition theory, then mechanistically, with explicit food on the table: with one resource, whoever survives on less of it wins; with two, the species can divide the world between them. Part IV changes the relationship — the second species is now the diner, not a rival — and then puts competition and predation in the same community, where everything learned separately stops being reliable. Part V steps back from the dynamics to the question of description: a community of many species, the two numbers ecologists use to sum it up, the samples those numbers are really computed from, and drift that changes them with no species better than another. Part VI spreads life across a landscape of sites. First it asks how a region's diversity is built from its sites' — how much comes from each site being rich, and how much from the sites being different — and what happens when the sites drift apart. Then it stops counting individuals altogether: a population becomes a single occupied dot on a landscape of patches, every one of those local populations is certain to go extinct, and the question becomes how the set of them persists anyway.

By the end of Part IV the honest answer to "what stops this population from growing?" is that you cannot tell by looking at the population, or even at the pair — you have to look at the whole community. Part V asks how such a community can be described in a number at all, and Part VI asks the question of a scale above it, where the answer turns out not to need the community, or the individuals, at all.

Working through the rooms in order is recommended, but each one stands on its own.

One word to watch: in Parts I and II, competition means competition among members of a single species — the crowding that regulates it from within. From Part III on it usually means competition between species. The rooms keep the two apart, and which one dominates decides every coexistence result in the lab.

The Growth Room

Unlimited growth – Nothing pushes back, so growth never stops. The population that cannot exist.

zoo_e lab

Start here

What it shows. A population in which nothing interferes: every individual has the same chance of giving birth and the same chance of dying, however crowded the world becomes. Births and deaths are drawn at random for each individual, so you see both the smooth exponential prediction and the noisy reality around it.

Try this. Run the defaults and switch the vertical axis from linear to logarithmic — the curve becomes a straight line, which is the signature of exponential growth. Then set the birth rate just barely above the death rate and run 20 replicate populations starting from 5 individuals. Finally, switch Compare two populations on and give population 2 a slightly higher death rate: two runs, identical in every other respect, on the same chart.

Predict first. Before you run twenty replicates with the birth rate just above the death rate, decide how many of the twenty will go extinct.

What this model assumes

  • Nothing gets in the way. Every individual has the same chance of giving birth and the same chance of dying however crowded the world becomes: b and d do not depend on N. This is the assumption the rest of the lab exists to break. Lifted in the room, where both rates bend with density.
  • The environment is inexhaustible. There is no food in this model, no space, nothing that can be used up — which is why the curve has nothing to level off against. Lifted in the room, where the ceiling is something the environment supplies.
  • Individuals are interchangeable. No age, no sex, no size, no genotype: one number describes the population completely. Real populations have structure, and a thousand newborns behave nothing like a thousand adults.
  • Births and deaths fall where they will, in continuous time. Each individual's events are drawn independently of every other's — no seasons, no breeding pulse, no generations arriving together. Lifted in the room, where the whole population turns over at once.
  • Nothing arrives and nothing leaves. The population is closed. Immigration is one of the commonest reasons a small real population persists when a model says it should not.
  • The rates never change. b and d are fixed for the whole run: no good years and bad years, no drought, no epidemic.

What to notice

The per-capita growth rate is a flat line: an individual in a crowd of ten thousand does exactly as well as one in a crowd of ten. That is the assumption this room rests on, and it is the assumption every later room breaks. Notice too that with a small starting population some replicates go extinct even though the average is growing — a positive growth rate is not a guarantee, because chance acts on individuals rather than on averages.

Where this goes next. The room makes individuals get in each other's way, which is the smallest possible repair to this model. Every other room in the lab is a different answer to the question this one leaves open: what actually stops the curve?

Questions to discuss

  1. Why can a population with a positive growth rate still go extinct?
  2. Why does exponential growth become a straight line on a logarithmic axis?
  3. What would have to change about the flat per-capita line for the population to stop growing?

What this model assumes

  • Crowding acts instantly. The rates at any moment depend on N at that same moment, so the population feels the crowd the instant it forms. Nothing in nature is that prompt. Lifted in the room, where the feedback is delayed by exactly one generation.
  • K belongs to the species. Carrying capacity is a number you set on a slider rather than something produced by anything, which makes it a description of the outcome and not an explanation of it. Lifted in the room, where K falls out of the bookkeeping instead.
  • Every individual competes equally with every other. Competition is a mean field: no territories, no neighbourhoods, nobody better at it than anybody else. Real competition is local, and local competition is weaker than this.
  • The density dependence is a straight line. Per-capita growth falls linearly from r at N = 0 to zero at K. Nothing obliges nature to be linear, and the shape matters: the Overshoot Room's three maps are three different shapes of this same idea, and they do not behave alike. It is also the only reason the fastest growth falls exactly at K/2 — bend the line and the peak moves with it.
  • Only this species counts against K. Nothing else is eating the same food or taking up the same room. Lifted in the and rooms.
  • Nothing eats them. Every death here is a death from crowding. Lifted in the room.

What to notice

Growth is fastest not when the population is small and not when it is large, but exactly halfway, at K/2 — the peak of the hump in the dN/dt panel. In this model that peak is the largest surplus a harvest could take forever, and it is the origin of the textbook idea of holding a fish stock near half its carrying capacity. But the halfway point belongs to the logistic model, not to populations: it follows from crowding that bites in a straight line, and with any other shape the peak moves. Read it as what this model predicts, not as a management rule — real stocks fluctuate, age, and are counted with error, and fishing at a calculated maximum has repeatedly proved too much. Notice also that K is an attractor: the population converges on it from above as readily as from below. And with an Allee effect, a population that falls below the threshold does not merely grow slowly — it goes extinct.

Where this goes next. This room treats K as a given number belonging to the species. The room takes that apart and shows where K actually comes from; the room keeps the same density dependence but delays it by one generation; and the room adds a second species that also counts against K.

Questions to discuss

  1. A deer population is held at half its carrying capacity by hunting. Is it growing fastest, slowest, or in between?
  2. Why is K called an attractor?
  3. Why is being rare dangerous for a species with an Allee effect?

Exactly what this room simulates

The deterministic logistic is integrated with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme. With demographic noise on, births and deaths are instead drawn as independent random events with density-dependent rates b(N) = b₀ − (r/2K)·N and d(N) = d₀ + (r/2K)·N, which have the logistic as their mean. K is an emergent balance point, not a wall: nothing prevents the population from exceeding it, it simply shrinks when it does. With two populations running, each is an entirely separate model — they share only the clock, the noise setting and the number of replicates, and never interact. (Two populations that do feel each other are the Neighbours Room.)

The Resource Room

Where K comes from – Food is supplied, eaten and runs short, and the ceiling is whatever falls out of the bookkeeping.

zoo_e lab

Start here

What it shows. The room treated K as a given. Here it is not: the environment supplies a resource at a fixed rate, consumers eat it, and their births depend on how much they ate. Both the resource and the consumer are tracked over time, and the population's ceiling emerges from the arithmetic rather than being imposed.

Try this. Run the defaults and watch the resource crash as the consumers build up, then settle. Read off R* — the resource level the consumers grind the environment down to. Now double the supply rate and run again. Finally, switch on Environment shift to cut the supply halfway through a run.

Predict first. Before you double the supply, decide what will happen to R* and to the number of consumers.

dR/dt = S − l·R − a·R·N
dN/dt = N·(e·a·R − m)
The resource is supplied at rate S, decays at rate l, and is consumed in proportion to how much of it there is and how many consumers there are. Consumers convert what they eat into offspring with efficiency e and die at rate m. Nothing here is a carrying capacity — and yet one appears.
What each symbol means
R
How much resource is standing there at that moment — food, nutrient, prey biomass. Unlike the last room, it is tracked explicitly.
N
The number of consumers eating it.
S
Supply: resource added per unit of time, from outside and regardless of anything else in the model.
l
Loss rate: the fraction of the standing resource that decays, washes away or is otherwise lost per unit of time, with nobody eating it.
a
Attack rate: how much resource one consumer takes per unit of time, per unit of resource present. It is the meeting rate between a consumer and its food.
e
Conversion efficiency: new consumers made per unit of resource eaten. Eating is not the same as breeding, and e is the exchange rate between them.
m
The consumers' per-capita death rate, constant and independent of how well they are eating.
R*
Not in the equations, but what falls out of them: R* = m/(e·a), the resource level at which a consumer exactly breaks even. It is what the population draws the resource down to, and it depends on nothing about the supply.

What this model assumes

  • The resource does not reproduce. It is abiotic — supplied at a constant rate, decaying at a constant rate, like a nutrient in a chemostat. A real food supply is usually itself a population with growth of its own. Lifted in the room, where what is eaten is a population that grows back.
  • A consumer eats in strict proportion to what is there. The functional response is linear: no handling time, no getting full, no upper limit on the rate at which one consumer can eat. Lifted in the room, where a saturating response changes the answer.
  • One consumer and one resource. The whole environment is a single thing that can run short. Lifted in the room, where two consumers meet one resource and then two.
  • Conversion is a constant. Every unit eaten becomes the same amount of consumer, at efficiency e, whatever the consumer's condition and however much it has already eaten.
  • Everything is well mixed. The resource is at the same concentration everywhere, and every consumer meets it at that concentration. Patchiness is the first thing a real environment has.
  • Mortality is a constant. m does not rise when the consumer is starving or when it is crowded — the only thing that can kill this consumer is arithmetic.

What to notice

Doubling the supply roughly doubles the number of consumers but leaves R* exactly where it was: R* depends only on the consumer's own mortality and efficiency, not on how rich the environment is. This is the single most useful fact in the whole lab. Notice too that "carrying capacity" is not a property of a species at all — the same consumer has a different K in every environment.

Exactly what this room simulates

A MacArthur-style consumer–resource model with an abiotic, chemostat-like resource: supplied at a constant rate S, lost at rate l, and eaten with a linear (type I) functional response. Whether it arrives there in a damped oscillation or slides in without ever overshooting depends on the parameters: linearising about the equilibrium gives complex eigenvalues, and so a spiral, only when S² < 4·m·R*·(S − l·R*), which requires the consumer to die faster than the resource decays (m > l).

What this model assumes

  • The resources are abiotic and independent. Each is supplied and decays at its own constant rate, neither grows, and neither affects the other.

What this model assumes

  • One predator, eating both competitors and nothing else. The whole community is three species. Every real food web this idea was drawn from has more, and indirect effects travel further in a larger one than they can here.
  • The predator's preference is a fixed attack rate. It does not switch to whichever competitor happens to be commoner. Switching is exactly the behaviour that can turn a keystone predator into an ordinary one, or the reverse, and it is not in these equations.
  • The predator never gets full. A linear functional response, so predation pressure rises without limit as prey become abundant. The room shows what saturation does to that.
  • Competition is still a bare coefficient. Everything the Neighbours Room assumed about α binds here too, so this room inherits a summary where it might have had a mechanism. The room supplies the mechanism.
  • No space, and nowhere to hide. Everything meets everything, uniformly. Paine's real tidepools had rock, tide height and refuges, and where the predator could not reach, the competitive winner won.
  • Nothing evolves and nothing learns. No species can answer predation by changing how it competes, growing a defence, or moving. On the timescale of a real cascade, some of them do.

What to notice

A predator is neither automatically destructive nor automatically a keystone: it restructures a community only when it presses hardest on the species that was winning. H′ = −Σ pi ln pi   ·   D = Σ pi²
Two ways of boiling a whole community down to one number, from the share pi of individuals that belong to each species. Shannon's H′ measures how hard it is to guess the species of the next individual you catch; Simpson's D is the chance that two individuals caught at random belong to the same species. What each symbol means
S
Species richness: how many species are present, however rare. Also written ⁰D, the effective number of order zero, because it counts every species as one whatever its share.
pi
The proportion of all individuals that belong to species i. The shares add up to 1. Every index in this room is computed from these shares and nothing else — not from body size, biomass, or how different the species are from one another.
H′
Shannon's index, in natural logarithms (nats). It is 0 when one species holds everything and ln S when all S are equally common. It gives rare species more weight than Simpson's does.
D

J′

What this model assumes

  • A species is its count, and nothing else. Every index here is computed from the shares of individuals alone: a whale counts the same as a minnow, and two near-identical species count as much as two unrelated ones. Biomass, body size and how different the species are from each other are all invisible to H′ and D.
  • Every species is identified correctly. A real sample has individuals nobody can name, species split or lumped by mistake, and juveniles that look like nothing in the key.
  • Every individual is equally likely to be caught. Samples are drawn at random, with replacement, from a community far larger than the sample. Real nets, traps and quadrats catch some species much more readily than others, and that bias adds to the sampling error shown here rather than averaging out.
  • In the drift, every species is identical. Births, deaths and immigration are the same for all of them, so no species is better than another and nothing regulates any of them: this is Hubbell's neutral model. It is the opposite assumption to the and rooms, where the differences between species decide everything.
  • The community is a fixed number of individuals. Every death is replaced at once, so J never changes. No species can grow by finding unused space, only by taking it from another.
  • The pool never changes, and nothing new evolves. Immigrants come from the community you built, in its proportions, for ever. Hubbell's full model also lets new species arise; without that, a closed community can only ever lose species.

What to notice

Two communities can hold the same species and still differ enormously in diversity, because diversity is richness and evenness. The three effective numbers say so at a glance: they agree only for a perfectly even community, and the gap between them is the unevenness. They also disagree about rare species. Add one, and richness goes up by a whole species, Shannon's number by a little, and Simpson's hardly at all. Choosing an index is choosing how much the rare species should count.

Then notice what sampling does to each of them. Richness is the worst behaved: a sample can miss a species but never invent one, so it is always too low, and how low depends mostly on how hard you looked. Two samples of different size compared on richness are mostly comparing effort. Shannon's number is biased low too, though less; Simpson's is nearly right from a few dozen individuals, because the commonest species that decide it are the ones a small sample does catch.

Finally, the drift: with no immigration, diversity drains away with no cause at all. No species is better than any other, yet one after another is lost, and in the end one holds the whole community — which one is luck. A trickle of immigrants is enough to stop the slide, and the level it settles at depends on J and m, not on any of the species. How diverse a place is depends on how large and how connected it is, as well as on who lives there.

Exactly what this room simulates

The community is a list of abundances, one per species, each a whole number from 0 to 100; pi is each divided by their total. H′ = −Σpi ln pi in natural logarithms, D = Σpi², J′ = H′/ln S, and the effective numbers are Hill's of order 0, 1 and 2: S, eH′ and 1/D.

A sample is n individuals drawn independently, each belonging to species i with probability pi — sampling with replacement from a community much larger than the sample. Its estimates are the plug-in ones, the formulas above applied to the sample's own shares, which is what is usually done with field data; corrections exist (Miller–Madow for Shannon, Simpson's own n(n − 1) form for D, Chao1 for richness) and are not applied. The dashed accumulation line is the expected number of species after k individuals, Σ[1 − (1 − pi)k].

The drift is a Moran model. The run starts from the community scaled to J individuals, rounded by largest remainder so the total is exactly J. At each step one individual, chosen at random, dies; with probability m it is replaced by an immigrant drawn from the pool's shares, and otherwise by the offspring of another individual of the community, chosen at random. A generation is J such steps, and the community is recorded at 300 evenly spaced moments. With m = 0 the expected time for a species of share p to be lost or to take over is of the order of J generations, which is why the default run is about as long as the community is large. The indices are Shannon (1948), Simpson (1949) and Hill (1973); the case for effective numbers is Jost (2006); the neutral model is Hubbell (2001), here without speciation.

What this model assumes

  • β means one thing here: γ/α, in effective numbers. That is one convention for partitioning diversity, chosen because its three terms share a scale and β reads as a number of sites. It is not the only one. β is also defined additively, as γ − α, and as any of dozens of dissimilarity indices between pairs of sites — Jaccard's, Sørensen's, Bray–Curtis — which measure related but different things. A β from one convention cannot be compared with a β from another, and even within this one it differs between richness, Shannon and Simpson.
  • Every site is the same size and counts the same. γ pools the sites with equal weight, and α averages them equally. Real sites differ in area and in how many individuals they hold, and weighting them by size changes all three numbers — which is one reason published values of β are so hard to compare.
  • The sites are counted completely. Every individual in every site is known. A real survey samples each site, and that inflates β: each sample misses a different handful of rare species, so the sites look more different than they are. It is the room's lesson about richness, doubled — and worst of all for β measured by richness, where a single missed individual changes the count.
  • A species is its count, and nothing else. As in the Diversity room, two closely related species count as much as two unrelated ones, and β says nothing about how different the species that change between sites actually are.
  • In the drift, every species is identical, and the sites are neutral communities exactly like the Diversity room's: births, deaths and immigration the same for all. Nothing here makes a species belong in one site rather than another, so all the turnover the drift produces is chance. Real turnover is mostly the opposite — different conditions favouring different species, which is what the Gradient preset draws and the drift cannot make.
  • Sites exchange no individuals with each other. Every immigrant comes from one regional pool, the pooled landscape you built, which never changes. Sites next door are no more alike than sites far apart, because there is no next door: like the room's patches, these sites have no positions.
  • Small sites are no more dangerous than large ones, individual for individual. In the drift a species is lost only by chance, never because a small site has too little habitat for it, harsher edges, or more frequent disasters. Those are the things that make the real answer to "several small or single large?" lean towards large, and none of them is here.

What to notice

Local diversity and regional diversity are different numbers, and one does not predict the other. A landscape of identical sites holds no more species than any one of them, however many sites there are; a landscape of distinct sites holds N times as many as a typical one. Drag the bars until two landscapes have the same α and different γ — it is easy — and a table of site diversities, however long, says nothing about the region: it takes β. Notice too that the measure matters, and which way it goes says something. Under Nested, β is largest by richness: the sites differ only in their rare species, which Shannon and Simpson discount. Under Gradient it is largest by Simpson: each site's commonest species is its own, while the rare tails overlap from one site to the next.

Then watch the drift make turnover out of nothing. Every site starts with the same species in the same proportions; each loses species by chance, and they do not lose the same ones, so β climbs while α falls. Immigration from a shared pool works against it, pulling every site back towards the same community — and if the sites start out different, under Its own row, immigration erases that difference too. Mixing makes places locally richer and regionally the same.

Finally, the comparison in the middle panel. Splitting the same number of individuals into several isolated sites makes each one lose species faster, but not the same species, so together they usually hold more than one large site. With no immigration they always do in the end: each small site finishes with a species of its own, and the large site with a single one. The large site often leads for a while early on, when the small sites have each lost their rare species and it has not yet lost its own — but under pure chance, several small comes out ahead. That is not the answer conservation usually reaches, and the difference is the point: what makes small sites risky in reality — edges, species that need a large area, a single disaster — is everything this model leaves out.

What this model assumes

  • A patch is occupied or empty, and nothing else. There is no population size inside a patch, so a patch holding three individuals is in exactly the same state as one holding three thousand — and is exactly as likely to go extinct. Every room before this one was about the difference between those two patches. The and rooms are what this model has deliberately thrown away.
  • Every patch can be reached from every other. Patches have no positions, so distance costs nothing and a coloniser is as likely to cross the landscape as to hop next door.   • Extinction risk does not depend on how full the neighbourhood is. Immigrants found new populations here, and never top up an existing one. When they do, extinction falls as p rises — the rescue effect — and occupancy can become all-or-nothing rather than settling gently at p*.
  • All patches are the same size and quality. One e, one c, for every patch in the landscape. Real patch networks are usually carried by a few large, reliable ones, and a model that averages them away will say a network is safe when the loss of one patch would end it.
  • Colonisation and extinction are instantaneous. A patch is recolonised the moment a coloniser arrives, with no founding phase in which the new population is small and likely to fail. That is the room's lesson about small numbers, left out here.
  • The landscape does not change while the model runs. Destroyed habitat is destroyed before the first frame. Habitat lost gradually leaves a metapopulation sitting above the occupancy its landscape can now support, and paying it off slowly — an extinction debt this room cannot show.

What to notice

The steady fraction is made of nothing steady. Park the scrubber after the curve flattens, step forward, and watch: p holds while patches wink out and are refounded underneath it, and the two counters keep climbing to the end of the run. Persistence at the landscape scale is not the absence of extinction — it is extinction being outrun.

Notice what p* is made of, too. Only the ratio e/c appears in it: double both rates and the equilibrium does not move, though the turnover beneath it doubles. And a species can be driven out of a landscape in which every patch remains perfectly habitable and every local population is exactly as robust as it ever was — set e just above c and nothing is wrong anywhere except in the arithmetic of the whole.

Where this comes from. This room inverts the lab's unit. The and rooms asked what happens inside one population; here each of those is reduced to a single bit, and the question becomes what happens to a population of them. The room's lesson returns in a new form: as there, the ceiling is not a property of the species but of what the environment supplies — and taking habitat away lowers it one for one.

Questions to discuss

  1. How can a set of populations persist when every one of them goes extinct?
  2. Why can a species vanish from a landscape with plenty of habitat left?
  3. Destroying 60% of the patches is fatal here, though the species occupied only 60% of them to begin with. Why?

Exactly what this room simulates

Patches are simulated individually rather than through the equation. Each sub-step, every occupied patch goes extinct with probability 1 − e−e·dt and every empty habitable patch is colonised with probability 1 − e−c·p·dt, both evaluated against the occupancy at the start of that sub-step so that a patch colonised within a step cannot also send colonists within it. Sub-steps are chosen to keep the per-patch probability per step below about 5%. Destroyed patches are assigned before the run at an even stride through the index and take no part in anything; they remain in the denominator of p. Both h and p₀ are rounded to whole patches and everything downstream — p*, the dashed curve, the two rate panels — is computed from the rounded values, so the prediction is about the landscape that exists rather than the one the sliders asked for; on a small landscape the two can differ visibly, and at the default hundred patches they do not differ at all. The dashed curve is the deterministic dp/dt = c·p(1 − h − p) − e·p integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta from the same p₀. Replicates share every parameter and differ only in their draws. The model is Levins (1969); the habitat-destruction term and its p* = 1 − h − e/c are Lande (1987).
