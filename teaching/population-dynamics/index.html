Population Laboratories

Welcome — start here.

zoo_e lab

Welcome to the Population Laboratories. Eight rooms of interactive simulations about a single question: what stops a population from growing? The first four answer it from inside the species — crowding, resources, and time lags. The last four answer it from outside: two rooms where another species eats the same food, one where another species eats you, and one where both things happen at once and the answer stops being obvious.

How to use the rooms

Every room follows the same pattern: set the parameters with the sliders and buttons at the top, press Run, and read the panels that fill in below. The whole trajectory is computed the moment you press Run and then played back to you, so you can pause anywhere, drag the History Scrubber at the bottom of most rooms back and forth, and replay any moment you like. In the Overshoot Room it is worth using deliberately: stepping one generation at a time is what turns the cobweb diagram from a tangle into an explanation.

Each room carries its own guidance, so this page stays short. A Start here panel — the first thing in the room, above everything else — says what the room shows and suggests what to try first; a What to notice panel below the simulation says what the run was for, and points to the other rooms that take the idea further. Follow those pointers and the eight rooms read as one argument rather than eight separate toys.

Between the two sits the equation the room is running. Underneath it is a collapsible What each symbol means key: open it and every letter in the equation above is named, said plainly, and placed against what the earlier rooms called it. Nothing in a room requires reading the algebra — but nothing in the algebra is left unexplained either.

Some rooms are deterministic: the same settings always give exactly the same curve, because the equations contain no chance. Others draw births and deaths at random, so running twice will not give the same answer twice — the parameters set tendencies, never outcomes. Each room says which kind it is.

The small red ? buttons beside a control or a panel explain what it does, what the numbers mean, and what to look for. If something on screen is unclear, there is usually a ? next to it.

Words worth pinning down

These seventeen come up again and again. Each one is underlined wherever it appears in a room — click it, anywhere in the tool, for a short explanation. They are gathered here too:

  • Population — a group of the same species, in one place, that could interbreed — the unit everything here is measured in.
  • Per-capita rate — per individual, per unit of time. Ask of every rate whether it is this or a population total.
  • Density dependence — a birth or death rate that changes with crowding. Only this can regulate a population.
  • Intrinsic rate of increase (r) — the per-capita growth rate of a population that nothing is holding back.
  • Carrying capacity (K) — the size at which births balance deaths, so the population stops changing.
  • Equilibrium — a state in which nothing is changing — and the real question is whether it attracts.
  • Intraspecific vs. interspecific competition — competition within a species versus between species. The balance between them decides every coexistence result in the lab.
  • Demographic stochasticity — the coin-flip nature of individual births and deaths, which matters most when numbers are small.
  • Phase plane — a graph of one species against another, with time nowhere on it.
  • Isocline (nullcline) — the line on a phase plane along which one species stops changing.
  • Invasion criterion — can this species increase when it is rare? Ask it of both species and you have the outcome.
  • Competitive exclusion — two species making a living in exactly the same way cannot persist together.
  • R* — the resource level at which a consumer breaks even; the lower one wins.
  • Functional response — how many prey one predator eats, as a function of how many there are.
  • Limit cycle — a permanent oscillation whose size belongs to the parameters, not to where the run started.
  • Trophic cascade — an effect that travels down a food chain and changes a level the predator never touches.
  • Niche — the conditions and resources a population needs to persist. Two species with identical niches cannot coexist.

The through-line

The eight rooms fall into four parts, and each part dismantles an assumption the one before it was allowed to make. Part I takes a single population on its own: first with nothing in its way, which turns out to be impossible for long, and then with individuals getting under each other's feet, which bends the curve into the familiar S. Part II asks where that ceiling actually comes from — not a number belonging to the species, but the resource the environment can supply — and then shows that even a perfectly self-limiting population need not settle down quietly: delay the feedback by one generation and it can cycle forever, or become genuinely unpredictable while obeying a perfectly deterministic rule. Part III adds a second species that wants the same food, first as a bare coefficient that produces the four classic outcomes of competition theory, then mechanistically, with explicit food on the table: with one resource, whoever survives on less of it wins; with two, the species can divide the world between them. Part IV changes the relationship — the second species is now the diner, not a rival — and then puts competition and predation in the same community, where everything learned separately stops being reliable.

By the last room the honest answer to "what stops this population from growing?" is that you cannot tell by looking at the population, or even at the pair. You have to look at the whole community.

Working through the rooms in order is recommended, but each one stands on its own.

One word to watch: in Parts I and II, competition means competition among members of a single species — the crowding that regulates it from within. From Part III on it usually means competition between species. The rooms keep the two apart, and which one dominates decides every coexistence result in the lab.

The Growth Room

Unlimited growth – Nothing pushes back, so growth never stops. The population that cannot exist.

zoo_e lab

Start here

What it shows. A population in which nothing interferes: every individual has the same chance of giving birth and the same chance of dying, however crowded the world becomes. Births and deaths are drawn at random for each individual, so you see both the smooth exponential prediction and the noisy reality around it.

Try this. Run the defaults and switch the vertical axis from linear to logarithmic — the curve becomes a straight line, which is the signature of exponential growth. Then set the birth rate just barely above the death rate and run 20 replicate populations starting from 5 individuals. Finally, switch Compare two populations on and give population 2 a slightly higher death rate: two runs, identical in every other respect, on the same chart.

What to notice

The per-capita growth rate is a flat line: an individual in a crowd of ten thousand does exactly as well as one in a crowd of ten. That is the assumption this room rests on, and it is the assumption every later room breaks. Notice too that with a small starting population some replicates go extinct even though the average is growing — a positive growth rate is not a guarantee, because chance acts on individuals rather than on averages.

Where this goes next. The room makes individuals get in each other's way, which is the smallest possible repair to this model. Every other room in the lab is a different answer to the question this one leaves open: what actually stops the curve?

Exactly what this room simulates

Births and deaths are drawn independently for every individual in every time step (a Poisson birth–death process), so replicate populations with identical parameters follow different paths. On the linear axis the chart is drawn up to 200 000 individuals: a population that outgrows it leaves through the top of the panel as a dotted line and keeps climbing, since nothing in the model ever stops it. The logarithmic axis has no such ceiling — it stretches to whatever the run reached, however many powers of ten that takes. The dot field shows one dot per individual up to 1 500, and one dot per several after that; with two populations on the panel each gets its own band and its own dot scale.

The Crowding Room

Intraspecific competition – Every individual added makes life slightly worse for the rest, until growth stops on its own.

zoo_e lab

Start here

What it shows. The same population, but now individuals get in each other's way — intraspecific competition. The birth rate falls and the death rate rises as the population grows; where the two lines cross, births exactly balance deaths and the population stops changing. That crossing point is the carrying capacity, K.

Try this. Run the defaults and watch the S-shaped curve level off at K. Then start the population above K and run again. Finally, open More parameters, switch on the Allee effect, and start with a very small population. To see one parameter's effect on its own, switch Compare two populations on and change nothing but K between them.

dN/dt = r·N·(1 − N/K)
The logistic equation. The bracket is the fraction of the environment still unused: it equals 1 in an empty world and 0 when the population reaches K. Growth is exponential when rare and vanishes at the carrying capacity.

Press Run. Watch the gap between the logistic curve and the pale exponential open up: that gap is everything the population failed to add because of its own numbers.

What to notice

Growth is fastest not when the population is small and not when it is large, but exactly halfway, at K/2 — that hump in the dN/dt panel is why fisheries aim to hold stocks near half of carrying capacity. Notice also that K is an attractor: the population converges on it from above as readily as from below. And with an Allee effect, a population that falls below the threshold does not merely grow slowly — it goes extinct.

Where this goes next. This room treats K as a given number belonging to the species. The room takes that apart and shows where K actually comes from; the room keeps the same density dependence but delays it by one generation; and the room adds a second species that also counts against K.

Exactly what this room simulates

The deterministic logistic is integrated with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme. With demographic noise on, births and deaths are instead drawn as independent random events with density-dependent rates b(N) = b₀ − (r/2K)·N and d(N) = d₀ + (r/2K)·N, which have the logistic as their mean. K is an emergent balance point, not a wall: nothing prevents the population from exceeding it, it simply shrinks when it does. With two populations running, each is an entirely separate model — they share only the clock, the noise setting and the number of replicates, and never interact. (Two populations that do feel each other are the Neighbours Room.)

The Resource Room

Where K comes from – Food is supplied, eaten and runs short, and the ceiling is whatever falls out of the bookkeeping.

zoo_e lab

Start here

What it shows. The room treated K as a given. Here it is not: the environment supplies a resource at a fixed rate, consumers eat it, and their births depend on how much they ate. Both the resource and the consumer are tracked over time, and the population's ceiling emerges from the arithmetic rather than being imposed.

Try this. Run the defaults and watch the resource crash as the consumers build up, then settle. Read off R* — the resource level the consumers grind the environment down to. Now double the supply rate and run again. Finally, switch on Environment shift to cut the supply halfway through a run.

dR/dt = S − l·R − a·R·N
dN/dt = N·(e·a·R − m)
The resource is supplied at rate S, decays at rate l, and is consumed in proportion to how much of it there is and how many consumers there are. Consumers convert what they eat into offspring with efficiency e and die at rate m. Nothing here is a carrying capacity — and yet one appears.
What each symbol means
R
How much resource is standing there at that moment — food, nutrient, prey biomass. Unlike the last room, it is tracked explicitly.
N
The number of consumers eating it.
S
Supply: resource added per unit of time, from outside and regardless of anything else in the model.
l
Loss rate: the fraction of the standing resource that decays, washes away or is otherwise lost per unit of time, with nobody eating it.
a
Attack rate: how much resource one consumer takes per unit of time, per unit of resource present. It is the meeting rate between a consumer and its food.
e
Conversion efficiency: new consumers made per unit of resource eaten. Eating is not the same as breeding, and e is the exchange rate between them.
m
The consumers' per-capita death rate, constant and independent of how well they are eating.
R*
Not in the equations, but what falls out of them: R* = m/(e·a), the resource level at which a consumer exactly breaks even. It is what the population draws the resource down to, and it depends on nothing about the supply.

Press Run. Watch the two panels side by side: every time the consumer curve turns over, the resource is crossing its R* line at that same instant. That is not a coincidence — it is the second equation with dN/dt set to zero.

What to notice

Doubling the supply roughly doubles the number of consumers but leaves R* exactly where it was: R* depends only on the consumer's own mortality and efficiency, not on how rich the environment is. This is the single most useful fact in the whole lab. Notice too that "carrying capacity" is not a property of a species at all — the same consumer has a different K in every environment.

Where this goes next. The room turns R* into a rule for predicting who wins a competition. If you have not yet met that competition in its simpler, phenomenological form, visit the room first.

Exactly what this room simulates

A MacArthur-style consumer–resource model with an abiotic, chemostat-like resource: supplied at a constant rate S, lost at rate l, and eaten with a linear (type I) functional response. Equilibrium: R* = m/(e·a) and N* = (S − l·R*)/(a·R*). The consumer persists only if it can drive the resource down to R*, that is if S/l > R*. Whether it arrives there in a damped oscillation or slides in without ever overshooting depends on the parameters: linearising about the equilibrium gives complex eigenvalues, and so a spiral, only when S² < 4·m·R*·(S − l·R*), which requires the consumer to die faster than the resource decays (m > l).

The Overshoot Room

Delayed density dependence – This year's crowding fixes next year's numbers, and one generation of delay is enough to break the calm.

zoo_e lab

Start here

What it shows. Many organisms breed once a year: this year's crowding sets next year's numbers, and nothing can respond in between. That one-generation lag is enough to break the tidy approach to K. Three classic discrete-time models are offered — scramble competition (Ricker), contest competition (Beverton–Holt) and the discrete logistic — along with a cobweb diagram and a live bifurcation diagram.

Try this. Start with Ricker at a low growth rate and watch it glide into K. Raise the rate slowly and watch the approach start to wobble, then split into a two-year cycle, then four, then dissolve. Switch to Beverton–Holt at the same high rate. Then turn on Sensitivity test, which runs a second population differing by one thousandth of an individual.

What to notice

Nothing random has been added anywhere: these equations are perfectly deterministic, and the wild fluctuations at high growth rates are generated by the density dependence itself. Yet under chaos the two nearly identical populations diverge completely within a few dozen generations — so a population can be both entirely rule-governed and entirely unpredictable. Contest competition never does this, because no amount of crowding can make it overshoot.

Where this goes next. This is the lab's first source of permanent cycles, and it is an internal one: a single species arguing with its own past. The room produces cycles a second way, out of two species arguing with each other — comparing the two is worth the trip.

Exactly what this room simulates

Three discrete-time maps of intraspecific competition, each a different shape of density dependence. Ricker: N′ = N·exp(r(1 − N/K)), overcompensating. Beverton–Holt: N′ = R₀N / (1 + (R₀−1)N/K) with R₀ = e^r, exactly compensating and monotone for every parameter value. Discrete logistic: N′ = N + rN(1 − N/K), which is the May map and can send N below zero at high r — the run stops if it does. The bifurcation diagram is recomputed live: 400 growth-rate values, 300 generations discarded as transient, the next 120 plotted.

The Neighbours Room

Interspecific competition – Two self-limiting species in one place. Four outcomes are possible, and only one is coexistence.

zoo_e lab

Start here

What it shows. Two species, each self-limiting exactly as in the room, now also feel each other — interspecific competition. One coefficient per species says how much a competitor counts in the currency of one's own crowding: α = 2 means one individual of the other species is as suppressive as two of your own.

Try this. Step through the four presets — A wins, B wins, Coexistence, Founder control — and after each one look at the phase plane rather than the time series. Then, in the founder-control preset, click at different points in the phase plane to start the run from a different pair of abundances and see the outcome flip.

dNA/dt = rANA(1 − (NA + αABNB)/KA)
dNB/dt = rBNB(1 − (NB + αBANA)/KB)
Two logistic equations, joined by two numbers. αAB is how much one individual of B crowds A, measured in units of A's own individuals: α = 1 means the two species are interchangeable, α < 1 that a competitor matters less than a fellow member of your own species, α > 1 that it matters more.
What each symbol means
NA, NB
The sizes of the two competing species. Each has its own equation, and each equation is the logistic of the Crowding Room with one extra term inside the bracket.
rA, rB
Each species' own intrinsic rate of increase. They govern how fast the outcome arrives, and — this is the room's first surprise — not which outcome it is.
KA, KB
Each species' own carrying capacity: the size it would settle at with the other species absent.
αAB
The effect of B on A: how many of A's own individuals one individual of B is worth as a crowder. α = 1 means the two are interchangeable, α < 1 that a competitor gets in your way less than a member of your own species, α > 1 that it gets in your way more.
αBA
The same number in the other direction. The two are independent — a species can be badly hurt by one that it barely troubles in return.
NA + αABNB
A's crowding, with B's individuals converted into A's currency. Everything the model does comes from comparing this total with KA.

Invasion criteria

Ask of each species: if it were vanishingly rare while its competitor sat at its own carrying capacity, could it increase? Both answers yes means coexistence; both no means founder control; one of each means exclusion.

Step through the run to park exactly on a moment worth looking at — a peak, a crossing, a turning point. The slider also responds to the ← and → keys once you click it.

Press Run. Then change only the growth rates and run again: the winner will not change, only how long it takes to win.

What to notice

Which species wins does not depend on who grows faster: the r sliders change how quickly the outcome arrives but never what it is. What matters is the balance between each species' effect on itself and its effect on the other. Coexistence requires each species to limit itself more than it limits its rival — an α below 1 is this model's way of saying the two occupy a different niche. When the reverse is true, both are stable alone and neither can invade, so whoever arrives first keeps the site.

Where this goes next. The α values here are descriptive — they say that a species suppresses another, not how. The room replaces them with an explicit shared food supply and derives the same outcomes from a mechanism. The room keeps these equations exactly as they are and adds a predator that eats both competitors, which can reverse every answer this room gives.

Exactly what this room simulates

Lotka–Volterra competition, integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta; a species is declared extinct and pinned to zero when it drops below 0.5 individuals. The isoclines are the straight lines along which each species' growth is exactly zero: N_A = K_A − α_AB·N_B and N_B = K_B − α_BA·N_A. Their arrangement, not the growth rates, decides the outcome.

The Coexistence Room

Competitive exclusion & niche partitioning – Both species eat the same tracked food, so competition is a shared dinner rather than a coefficient.

zoo_e lab

Start here

What it shows. The room's competition coefficients are descriptive: they say that a species suppresses another, not how. Here both species eat explicit, tracked resources, so competition happens for a reason. In one resource mode there is a single food supply; in two resources mode there are two, and each species can specialise.

What to notice

With one resource the species with the lower R* always wins, no matter how abundant the other starts, how fast it grows, or how hungrily it eats: it simply drives the resource below the level its rival needs to break even. This is the competitive exclusion principle, and R* explains it. With two resources, coexistence appears the moment each species is held back mainly by the resource it is better at using — which is what ecologists mean by partitioning a niche.

What to notice

The predator's peaks trail the prey's by a quarter of a cycle, always and for a reason: predators can only build up after their food has, and prey can only recover after the predators have starved. Look hard at the equilibrium too — the prey's equilibrium abundance N* = m/(a(e − m·h)) contains no prey parameters whatsoever. Feeding the prey population better cannot make it commoner; it makes predators commoner instead, and past a threshold it destabilises the pair entirely. Enrichment passing straight through one level to the one above it is the arithmetic behind a trophic cascade.

Where this goes next. The room made cycles out of one species and a time lag; this room makes them out of two species and a feedback, and the limit cycle you get from an enriched environment is a third kind again. The room drops this predator into the room's competition, where its effect on who survives is the opposite of what you would guess.

Exactly what this room simulates

Lotka–Volterra predation, integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta at sixteen sub-steps per frame, which holds the classic model's conserved quantity to about one part in 10¹⁰ over a run: the closed orbits on screen are closed in the mathematics, not by accident of the integrator. A population is declared extinct and pinned to zero below 0.5 individuals — the equations themselves permit a prey population of a hundredth of an individual to recover, and that floor is the room's one concession to reality. Prey released by their predator's extinction grow without limit: the time series stays scaled to the cycles and lets them leave through the top of the panel as a dotted line. The equilibrium is N* = m/(a(e − m·h)), P* = N·g(N*)/f(N*); its stability is decided by the sign of g(N*) + N*·g′(N*) − f′(N*)·P*, which is negative (stable) whenever the prey nullcline is falling where the predator nullcline crosses it.

What to notice

A predator is neither automatically destructive nor automatically a keystone: it restructures a community only when it presses hardest on the species that was winning. Drag the History Scrubber slowly through the start of a keystone run and watch the phase plane — the competitors' isoclines slide inward at different speeds, cross, and a coexistence appears where none existed. Notice too what apparent competition does to fieldwork: two species whose abundances move in opposite directions look exactly like competitors, and need not be competing for anything at all. Both results here are indirect effects — one species changing another's fate along a path it never walks directly, which is what a trophic cascade does between trophic levels and apparent competition does within one.

Where this comes from. This room only makes sense against the room, whose outcome it overturns, and the room, whose predator it borrows. It is also the lab's last word on the opening question: by this point, "what stops this population from growing?" cannot be answered by looking at the population, or even at the pair — only at the whole community.

Exactly what this room simulates

Two Lotka–Volterra competitors and one shared predator with a linear functional response, integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta; any population below 0.5 individuals is pinned to zero. Competitor i invades a world holding competitor j and the predator when ri(1 − αijNj*/Ki) − aiPj* > 0, where (Nj*, Pj*) = (m/(e·aj), (rj/aj)(1 − Nj*/Kj)) is the state of a world containing only j and the predator. Both invasion criteria satisfied means coexistence of all three. The three-species equilibrium solves aANA + aBNB = m/e simultaneously with the two competitors' zero-growth conditions. Keystone predation is Paine (1966); apparent competition is Holt (1977).
